How the wheel campaign tracker works

The wheel rotates between a cash-secured put and a covered call. During the put phase, the investor reserves enough cash to buy 100 shares per contract if assigned. After assignment, the investor may sell a call against those shares. If the call is assigned, the shares are sold and the cycle can begin again.

The tracker adds the current option’s net premium to previous campaign credits. During the put phase, economic assignment price is the strike minus total credits allocated per share. During the call phase, economic share breakeven is the original share purchase or assignment price minus all allocated option credits and entered dividends per share.

Example: Earlier wheel trades produced $200. A new $48 put brings in $150 before $1 of fees. Total campaign credits become $349, or $3.49 per share. If assigned on 100 shares at $48, the campaign’s economic entry is $44.51. This does not change the contractual $48 assignment price or automatically become the broker’s tax basis.

Track one consistent share lot. If contracts, share count, or lots change, separate the campaigns or carefully allocate credits. Mixing realized gains from one lot with another can make the displayed breakeven look safer than the actual position.

How option roll calculations work

An option roll is two transactions: buy the existing short option to close, then sell another option to open. Net roll credit equals the new premium received minus the close cost and entered fees. A negative number is a net debit. Credits after the roll equal prior net campaign credits plus the current roll credit or debit.

For a covered call, the comparison shows how the new strike and net roll amount change profit if the shares are called away. Raising the strike can improve the sale price even when the roll costs a debit; lowering it can reduce called-away profit despite a credit. For a cash-secured put, the new strike and total credits determine the premium-adjusted assignment price.

Example: Buying back one call for $2.50 and selling the new call for $3.40 collects $90 before fees. If the roll also raises the strike from $52 to $55, the called-away outcome improves by the $300 strike difference plus the net roll credit. That arithmetic does not mean the roll has positive expected value or removes downside risk in the shares.

Risks, assignment, and recordkeeping limitations
  • Rolling realizes the gain or loss on the old option and opens a new obligation. It does not erase a loss.
  • American-style short options can be assigned before expiration. A multi-leg roll order may not execute at the displayed net price.
  • A covered call still carries nearly all downside risk of owning the shares and caps appreciation above its strike.
  • A cash-secured put can create a substantial loss if the assigned shares fall well below the strike.
  • Annualized roll returns can exaggerate small short-term credits and assume the result can be repeated.
  • “Economic breakeven” is a planning measure. Tax basis, holding periods, wash-sale treatment, qualified covered-call rules, straddle rules, and assignment reporting can differ.

Reference: the Options Industry Council describes the wheel and rolling covered calls and explains that assignment can occur on any business day while an American-style short option remains open.

Common questions

What is the net credit or debit on a roll?

The new option premium received minus the cost to close the old option and all entered fees. A positive result is a credit; a negative result is a debit.

Does premium reduce my tax cost basis?

Not necessarily. NumberCove shows an economic campaign breakeven. Broker-reported tax treatment depends on whether options expire, close, or are assigned, along with holding-period and other tax rules.

Does rolling prevent assignment?

No. Closing the old short option ends that option’s future assignment exposure only after the close executes and no prior assignment has occurred. The new short option begins a new assignment risk.
