Money · Loans & mortgages

Pay off the mortgage early or invest?

Extra payments earn a guaranteed return equal to your mortgage rate. Investing might earn more. See exactly where the line is with your numbers.

Your mortgage

The answer

Enter your numbers to see the answer.

How this calculator works

Two strategies

  • Pay off early: add the extra amount to every payment until the loan is gone, then invest the full payment plus the extra for the rest of the original term.
  • Invest the extra: keep the regular schedule and invest the extra every month.

Both end at the original payoff date with no mortgage, so the comparison is the investment balance.

Returns and taxes

The investment return is an annual rate converted to monthly. Tax drag lowers it for taxable accounts. If you itemize, the interest deduction's tax savings are invested in both strategies.

Break-even return

The calculator searches for the return at which both strategies end with the same net worth. Because a mortgage rate compounds monthly, the break-even annual return is slightly above the stated rate (a 6% mortgage equals about 6.17% a year).

Sources

  1. IRS Publication 936: Home mortgage interest deduction

Last reviewed 2026-10-27. Rules, rates, and prices change; check the source for your exact situation.

Common questions

Is it better to pay off my mortgage early or invest?

If your investments earn more than your mortgage rate after taxes, investing builds more wealth on average. Paying off the mortgage is a guaranteed return with no market risk. The calculator shows the break-even return for your loan.

What is the break-even return?

The annual investment return at which investing the extra and paying off the loan early leave you with the same net worth at the original payoff date.

Does the mortgage interest deduction change the answer?

Only if you itemize deductions. It lowers your effective mortgage rate, which makes investing more attractive.

What the default example really says

The calculator starts with a $300,000 balance at 6.5% and 28 years left, with $500 a month to put toward either the mortgage or investments. Paying extra clears the loan in 203 months, about 17 years, and saves $157,068 in interest. Investing the $500 at an assumed 7% instead ends $16,533 ahead at the original payoff date.

That win depends on earning more than 6.70% a year for 28 years. The margin is only 0.3 percentage points, and the outcomes aren't symmetric:

Average investment returnResult vs. paying off early
8%Investing ahead by $80,846
7% (default)Investing ahead by $16,533
6%Paying off ahead by $33,548
5%Paying off ahead by $72,108

A 1% tax drag on a taxable account has the same effect as a 1-point lower return: at 7% with 1% drag, paying off early wins by $33,548.

The mortgage rate decides most of the answer

Holding the 7% return assumption, the same $500 a month gives opposite answers depending on the loan:

  • 3% mortgage: investing ends $175,864 ahead.
  • 4% mortgage: investing ends $138,012 ahead.
  • 7.5% mortgage: paying off early ends $44,081 ahead, with no market risk.

Low-rate mortgages from earlier years are cheap money, and prepaying them gives up a lot of expected return. At today's higher rates, the guaranteed return from prepaying is much harder to beat.

What the calculator can't weigh for you

  • Liquidity. Money in investments can be sold in an emergency. Money paid into the house can come back out only by selling or borrowing against it, and lenders tighten up exactly when people need cash.
  • Higher-return steps that usually come first. Capturing a full 401(k) employer match is an immediate return that neither option here can match, and paying off credit card debt at 20% or more beats both. An emergency fund protects against having to sell investments at a bad time.
  • Risk you can live with. A paid-off house lowers the income you need every month, which has value in a job loss or in retirement. The calculator measures net worth, not peace of mind.
