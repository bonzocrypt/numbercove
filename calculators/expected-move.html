How the implied-volatility expected move works

The one-standard-deviation approximation is stock price × implied volatility × √(calendar days ÷ 365). Implied volatility must be entered as an annualized decimal in the formula, so 35% becomes 0.35. The calculator reports the result in dollars and as a percentage of the current stock price.

Under a simplified normal interpretation, a one-standard-deviation range contains roughly 68% of outcomes and a two-standard-deviation range roughly 95%. Actual stock returns are not normally distributed: gaps, volatility changes, skew, earnings announcements, takeovers, and market stress can produce outcomes well outside those ranges. The range is an options-pricing translation, not a confidence guarantee.

Example: A $100 stock with 35% annualized IV and 30 calendar days has an estimated one-standard-deviation move of about $10.04, producing a straight-line range near $89.96 to $110.04. Doubling the move gives a rough two-standard-deviation range, not a maximum possible move.

Reference: Fidelity’s options education material presents the equivalent square-root-of-time expected-move formula using trading days when the input period is measured in trading days. This calculator consistently uses calendar days and 365.

How the straddle method differs

An at-the-money straddle is the price of a call plus the price of a put at the same strike and expiration. The full straddle cost gives the approximate up-or-down distance required for a long straddle to break even at expiration, before fees and assuming the strike is near spot. Traders sometimes multiply that cost by a factor such as 0.85 to create a separate expected-move shortcut.

The calculator deliberately shows both numbers. The factor-based estimate is not identical to the IV one-standard-deviation range, and the full straddle cost is not a probability statement. Option prices contain supply and demand, volatility skew, event risk, rates, dividends, bid/ask spreads, and a volatility risk premium. Use mid-prices from a synchronized quote and verify that both options share the same expiration and strike.

Fidelity notes that call and put prices in a straddle reflect the options market’s consensus about movement before expiration. Cboe likewise describes comparing short-dated option prices around earnings, while warning that the options indicate magnitude rather than direction.

Choosing the right IV and time period
  • Use implied volatility from the expiration matching the event or forecast horizon. A 30-day IV should not be applied blindly to an overnight earnings move.
  • For an earnings event, near-term IV includes ordinary movement before and after the announcement as well as event volatility. Isolating the event requires comparing adjacent expirations or a term-structure model.
  • Use an at-the-money IV or a clearly defined blend. Put and call skew means there is no single IV describing every strike.
  • IV can change immediately after an event. A correct magnitude estimate does not guarantee that a long option position makes money because implied volatility and time value may fall.
  • The target comparison uses a simplified normal tail estimate. It does not model drift, skew, fat tails, or direction-specific probabilities.

Common questions

What formula does the calculator use?

Stock price × annualized IV × the square root of calendar days divided by 365 for the approximate one-standard-deviation move.

Does expected move predict direction?

No. It estimates magnitude. A ±$10 move does not say whether the stock is more likely to rise or fall.

Why does the straddle estimate differ from the IV result?

The straddle is a traded two-option package affected by skew, event pricing, bid/ask spreads and risk premium. Its full cost also represents a long straddle’s expiration breakeven distance, not necessarily one standard deviation.

Educational estimate only. Expected move is not a price target, guarantee, trading recommendation, or probability of profit. Options can lose their entire value, short options can create substantial obligations, and actual moves can exceed modeled ranges.
