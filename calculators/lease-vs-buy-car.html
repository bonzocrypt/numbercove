How this calculator works

Lease payment

A lease charges for the value the car loses while you drive it, plus a finance charge set by the money factor.

Adjusted cap cost = negotiated price + acquisition fee − down payment

Residual = MSRP × residual %

Payment = (adjusted cap − residual) ÷ months + (adjusted cap + residual) × money factor, plus sales tax on the payment

Money factor × 2,400 gives the equivalent APR.

Cost over the lease term

Lease cost = down payment (with tax) + payments + disposition fee + extra-mile charges

Buy cost = down payment + loan payments made − (car's value − loan balance left)

The car's value when the lease would end defaults to the residual value, the lender's own estimate.

Keeping it longer

Buying usually wins the longer you keep the car, because payments stop when the loan is paid off. The chart compares buying and keeping with leasing continuously at the same terms, with the car losing value at the same yearly rate and repairs added after the warranty ends.

Common questions

Is it better to lease or buy a car?

Buying and keeping a car for many years is usually cheapest, because you stop making payments once the loan is paid off. Leasing can make sense if you want a new car every few years, drive predictable miles, or get a subsidized lease with a high residual and low money factor.

How do I convert a money factor to an interest rate?

Multiply by 2,400. A money factor of 0.0025 is about 6% APR. Ask the dealer for the money factor and compare it with the lender's base rate; dealers can mark it up.

What is a good residual value?

Higher is better for a lease, since you pay only for the drop from your price to the residual. Many 36-month leases have residuals in the 50% to 60% range of MSRP, and the lender sets it.

Should I put money down on a lease?

Usually keep it small. A cap cost reduction lowers your payment, but if the car is totaled or stolen early, that down payment is typically lost, even with gap coverage.
