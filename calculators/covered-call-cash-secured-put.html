How covered call returns are calculated

A covered call combines long shares with a short call covering the same number of shares. Net premium equals option premium multiplied by 100 shares per contract, less entered trading fees. Expiration breakeven is the share cost basis minus net premium per share and any dividends included for the period.

If the stock finishes above the strike and the call is assigned, profit is the strike minus share cost basis, multiplied by shares, plus option premium and dividends. The call limits further stock appreciation above the strike. If the stock falls, premium provides only a small cushion; the position can still lose almost the entire stock investment.

Example: Buy or own 100 shares at $50, sell a $55 call for $2, and pay no fees. Breakeven is $48. If assigned at $55, profit is $700: $500 of stock appreciation plus $200 of premium. If the stock finishes at $40, the position loses $800 because the $1,000 stock decline is only partly offset by premium.

How cash-secured put returns are calculated

A cash-secured put reserves enough cash to buy 100 shares per contract at the strike. The maximum option profit is the net premium. The premium return shown here divides net premium by strike-price cash collateral; the annualized figure compounds that single-period return over a year and should not be interpreted as a promised repeatable yield.

Premium-adjusted assignment price is the strike minus net premium per share. If the stock finishes below the strike, expiration P&L is net premium minus the strike-to-market loss on the assigned shares. Maximum loss assumes the company reaches zero and is therefore substantial.

Example: Sell one $45 put for $1.50. The trade reserves $4,500, receives $150 before fees, and has a $43.50 breakeven. At a $40 expiration price, the position is down $350 even though the premium is retained.

Important assignment, dividend, and model limitations
  • American-style equity options can be assigned before expiration. Finishing in the money is not the same as receiving an assignment notice, and out-of-the-money assignment is still possible.
  • Covered calls may face increased early-assignment risk before an ex-dividend date when remaining time value is small.
  • The probability estimate uses a simplified risk-neutral lognormal model. It is not a forecast, ignores volatility skew, and is not the probability of profit.
  • Annualized returns assume compounding and do not include changing volatility, slippage, bid/ask spreads, taxes, margin interest, or reinvestment delays.
  • Standard equity options normally represent 100 shares, but adjusted contracts can have different deliverables.

Common questions

How is covered call breakeven calculated?

Share cost basis minus net call premium per share and any dividends included for the period. This is an expiration breakeven, not a tax-basis adjustment.

How is cash-secured put return calculated?

Net option premium divided by the strike-price cash reserved for assignment. The calculator also shows a compounded annualized equivalent for comparison.

Can either strategy lose money?

Yes. The premium is limited while the underlying stock can fall substantially. A covered call or assigned put can lose nearly all committed capital if the stock reaches zero.
